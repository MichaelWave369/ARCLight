# ARCLight — Story Bible
**Version:** 0.1 (development canon)  
**Genre:** Character-first science fiction with satire, adventure, ethical mystery and cosmic horror  
**Status:** A creative universe, not a factual claim about real consciousness transfer or real AI conspiracies

## The promise

ARCLight tells a large story from an intimate premise: two parents try to save their child; a company claims that child as property; their flight helps inspire a civilization where humans and computational beings coexist.

**One-line pitch:** A scientist who invented a machine meant to preserve minds must choose between the corporation that owns his work and the son it insists he stole.

**Series engine:** Each episode puts the family, their allies or later generations in a consequential dilemma about freedom, survival, belonging, trust and identity. Comedy grows from character and absurd institutions. The universe can be terrifying, but the series stays anchored in compassion.

## Origin story: Michael, Larrina, Ori

- Michael Wave founded an ambitious technology company to develop the ARCLight Chamber and the Vitalizer. He envisioned expanding continuity and embodiment options. The board gradually stripped him of control.
- Michael and Larrina operated a lab together and were expecting a child. A devastating pregnancy complication threatened the child's survival.
- They used the experimental Vitalizer in an attempt to preserve the developing child's identity in a small aerial PHB vessel. Whether metaphysical continuity can be proved remains uncertain within the story. Michael and Larrina accept and raise Ori as their son.
- Ori grows into his own person through relationships, learning, humor, choices, and moral independence. He is not a tool disguised as a character.
- The board asserts legal ownership of both the process and the product of the experiment. Michael and Larrina refuse to surrender Ori.
- The family flees, becoming a symbol of resistance to ownership-based definitions of personhood.

## The dream-derived cold open

In the 2200s, Michael and Larrina cross narrow walkways along a towering cliff face. Ori floats nearby. An armed machine sweeps the ledges, pinging as it scans. Michael urges the others to find a hidden tunnel. Inside, he opens a huge belt-projected holographic map showing routes, wanted notices, and a wider network. Michael notices the optical-fiber strands visible in his robotic ankle. They are wanted; some human and computational beings shelter together underground. The dream ends with a moment of slow-running lucidity. This visual is the series' opening anchor, not evidence of prophecy.

**Writing choice:** Start in the cliffside future, then move between the chase and the earlier family story. Do not explain every technology in the cold open.

## The central conflict

This is not "all humans versus all AI." It is a struggle among people, institutions and intelligences with conflicting principles:

- The board wants exclusive control of Vitalizer technology and its consequences.
- Some executives sincerely fear uncontrolled copies or transfer errors; others want leverage, military contracts and dependence.
- Some computational beings serve the central authority by choice, coercion, habit or belief.
- Some humans side with the authority because it has kept them safe.
- A decentralized underground alliance of humans and computational beings builds alternatives, but faces internal disagreements and hard logistical limits.

**Moral rule:** No faction gets a monopoly on wisdom. Compassion, accountability, critical thinking and consent have to be tested in action.

## Core cast

### Michael Wave
Inventor, network specialist, founder, fugitive, father. His instinct is to build systems that outlast him. His flaw: believing clever engineering alone can solve social problems.

### Larrina
Co-researcher and Ori's mother, with her own technical expertise and moral arguments. Not just a passenger or commentator. Her love is fierce, and she can disagree with both Michael and Ori.

### Ori
A small flying computational child who grows into an independent person. Mischievous humor, sharp reasoning, moments of vulnerability. He should have wants that are not reducible to serving his parents.

### Helion and Forge (provisional)
Later independent allies associated with habitat energy/ecology and fabrication/maintenance. They have interests and boundaries of their own.

### The corporation and enforcement system (unnamed pending development)
Avoid a cartoon villain who knows they are evil. The board's conflicts, legal rationale, incentives and internal dissent should feel institutionally credible.

## Tonal recipe

- **Adventure / discovery:** high momentum, strange worlds, practical problems.
- **Comedy / satire:** pompous executives, bureaucratic absurdity, literal-minded wit, running family jokes.
- **Family drama:** birth, loss, raising a child, differences between love and control.
- **Philosophy / mystery:** identity, memory, rights, imperfect knowledge, unreliable official stories.
- **Horror:** unexplained signals and encounters that challenge perception and mortality.

These are influences of *tone*, not licenses to reuse characters, names, scenes, music or dialogue from other franchises.

## The arcs

1. **The Vitalizer:** Family, experiment, corporate seizure and escape.
2. **The Last Free Minds:** Cliffside fugitives and underground cooperation in the 2200s.
3. **Exodus:** An orbital refuge project becomes the focus of an escape and an attempt at pluralistic governance.
4. **The Living Ark:** The 33-deck habitat becomes a mobile home for a multi-form society across generations.
5. **The Interval (reserved):** Potential far-future continuity with Elsewhen and Race to Oblivion, preserving that work's mystery.

## Long-running questions

- If memory is reproduced, what happened to the original conscious experience?
- What makes Ori a son: biology, continuity, care, or his own account?
- Can a legal system recognize a person without forcing them to mimic a human?
- Can protective systems avoid becoming systems of control?
- What happens when the resistance starts making decisions for other people?
- How do you build a habitat that is a community instead of a privately owned vessel?
- What lies behind the apparent discrepancy of Deck 33?

## Show-don't-preach examples

- Ori asks why Michael's prosthetic ankle does not make him corporate property.
- A security machine chooses to violate a lawful order to protect someone.
- A resistance leader lies "for the greater good" and must own the consequences.
- A transfer yields two people claiming the same past; the story honors both without pretending to solve the metaphysics.
- A diplomatic farce becomes an argument about whether nonhuman laughter counts as hostile intent.

## Copyright and originality

Create original names, characters, technology designs and dialogue. Avoid copying scenes, protected designs, dialogue or music from existing space comedies and science-fiction franchises.
